The Retraction Trade-off

Can you make "cross it off the list" reversible — without breaking everything else the list has to do?

The setup, in one minute

A guessing game where the answer might not be on your list

Picture Twenty Questions, but open-world: the hidden object might be something you've never seen. A program keeps a list of candidate objects, asks yes/no questions to narrow it down, and — when nothing on the list fits — calls an expert who names the new object and adds it. The list grows by discovery.

Everything turns on one choice: how do you represent "what it could still be"? There are three honest answers, and they behave very differently when the person answering your questions is occasionally wrong.

soft keep doubts

Never fully rule anything out — keep weighted probabilities and floor them so nothing hits exactly zero. Survives a wrong answer, but belief is a cloud: no crisp record of what's been eliminated.

hard cross off

Strike any candidate an answer contradicts, permanently. Crisp and auditable — but a single wrong answer crosses off the true object, and it can never come back.

retract cross off, take back

Strike like hard, but allow un-striking when later evidence recovers a candidate. The hoped-for best of both: crisp records and resilience.

The experiment runs all three as the answerer gets noisier — flipping 5% then 10% of answers — and measures what breaks.

Finding 1

Hard elimination breaks under noise — retraction recovers it

The key number is the in-pool failure rate: when the secret really was a known object (so it was findable), how often did the method fail to find it? With a perfect answerer every method is fine. As answers start flipping, hard falls apart — one flip crosses off the truth for good — while soft barely notices. retract lands between them, and with a generous enough "take-back" rule it climbs almost all the way back to soft.

Finding 2 — the catch

The same knob that recovers the truth also revives the wrongs

Retraction has one dial: how readily a crossed-off candidate is allowed back. Widen it and in-pool recovery improves (left). But widen it and the method starts reviving candidates that deserved to be crossed off — so it mis-guesses, and it stops recognizing genuinely new objects, calling the expert far less often (right). Crucially, this cost appears even with a perfect answerer, where nothing should ever be revived.

Read the two charts together: they move in opposite directions along the same dial. There is no setting that keeps both low. That tension — not any single number — is the finding.

Why it happens

A single score can't tell an unlucky truth from an ordinary wrong

When should a crossed-off candidate come back? This version decides with one number — a running score of how well each candidate fits the answers so far — and revives anything that climbs back within a margin of the leader. The problem is that two very different candidates produce the same dip in that score:

That is why the dial can't win: it is asked to make a distinction the information it looks at doesn't contain. The truth and the wrong differ in which answers hurt them and whether contradictions keep coming — not in how far a single score dropped.

What it points to

Remember what killed each candidate, not just a score

The fix isn't a better threshold — it's more structure. If each crossed-off candidate carries a record of which answer struck it, you can ask the question a single score can't: was this candidate killed by one lonely outlier (revive it) or contradicted again and again (leave it)? That's the difference between a truth unlucky with one flip and a wrong that simply doesn't fit.

This is the motivation for a structured belief store with per-candidate "tombstones" — an elimination tied to its cause, reversible by naming that cause. The scalar version here isn't a failure; it's the control that shows why the structure is needed. The next step measures whether that structure buys the resilience without the novelty-detection cost.

The one-line takeaway. Making elimination reversible with a single tunable score recovers robustness to noise but trades away the ability to spot genuinely new things — because one number can't distinguish an unlucky truth from an ordinary wrong. Telling them apart needs memory of why each candidate was eliminated.
